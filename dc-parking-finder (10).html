How the colors are decided

Every street block in the District is checked against DDOT's Residential Parking Permit (RPP) Blocks dataset. Green means that block is not in the RPP dataset, so the standard 2-hour non-resident limit does not apply there. Red means the block is flagged as an RPP zone. Amber dots mark individual parking meters, from DDOT's meter inventory; those load once you zoom in past street level.

On a red block

Without a DC-issued RPP sticker for that zone, or an active reciprocity permit, a vehicle can only stay up to 2 hours during enforcement hours (mostly weekdays; some zones, including Georgetown, also enforce evenings and Saturdays). Moving the car to another spot in the same zone does not reset the clock. DC tracks the plate across the whole zone, not just the parking space.

On a green block

There is no 2-hour RPP cap here, but green does not mean free. Check for meter dots before assuming a spot has no charge. This dataset also does not capture street cleaning schedules, snow-emergency routes, loading zones, rush-hour clearways, or plain "No Parking Anytime" signs. Always check the posted signage on the actual block before treating a spot as safe long-term.

The 72-hour rule

Regardless of color, DC does not allow a vehicle to sit in the same spot on a public street for more than 72 consecutive hours. It applies everywhere, RPP zone or not, metered or not, and it's enforced separately from RPP and meters as its own abandoned-vehicle rule. Move the car at least once every three days if you're leaving it somewhere for a while.

Other things that override everything

Temporary signs, usually paper zip-tied to a pole, go up for construction, moving trucks, and special events with little notice, and they supersede whatever's normally posted, RPP or not. Streets with rush-hour restrictions, commonly 7 to 9am and 4 to 6:30pm, tow immediately during those windows even if they're open parking the rest of the day. And two or more unpaid tickets older than 60 days make a vehicle eligible for booting, on top of whatever the underlying violation was. None of that shows up on this map, so it's worth a glance at the actual pole before you walk away.

Out-of-state plates

Anyone keeping an out-of-state vehicle in DC for an extended stretch may qualify for a DC DMV reciprocity permit, which allows the vehicle to keep its home-state plates while becoming eligible for an RPP sticker in whichever zone it's parked, removing the 2-hour cap on red blocks. Separately, DC generally requires a vehicle kept in the District to be registered after 60 consecutive days, and DPW can flag an unregistered out-of-state car after 30 days, unless it holds a reciprocity permit or exemption.

Data source

Streets, RPP blocks, and meters are pulled live from DC GIS (DDOT) each time you pan the map or tap Full city. A city-wide load pages through several thousand blocks, so it takes a few seconds longer than a single block, and meters only load once you're zoomed in close enough to make out individual blocks.
